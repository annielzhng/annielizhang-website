## What we did

I interviewed 24 scientists with large followings (10,000 or more followers on TikTok, Instagram, or X) across North America and Europe. I asked how they picture their audiences, how they present themselves, and how they handle the tension between professional norms and what platforms reward.

## What we found

- **They write for someone specific.** Each scientist had a target audience in mind, from peers to parents to skeptics, and adjusted their tone, jargon, and personal sharing to match.
- **Context collapse is real.** One post can reach many mismatched audiences, so many scientists self-censor, hedge, or write for the strictest audience, such as a future hiring committee.
- **Authenticity builds trust.** Being open about the process and owning mistakes builds credibility, though sharing more can invite one-sided, parasocial relationships with followers.
- **Humanizing helps, within limits.** Personal stories and humor counter the “cold scientist” stereotype, but some followers push back on posts that are not about science or that touch on politics.
- **Representation matters, and it costs.** Women and scientists from minoritized backgrounds use their visibility to mentor and to widen who belongs in STEM, while facing more harassment and emotional labor.

## Why it matters

Being a scientist online is more than sharing facts. Clear audience targeting, calibrated authenticity, and support from institutions can make this public work safer and more effective.
